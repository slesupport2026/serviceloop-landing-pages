The Homeowner Feed

Where electrician virality actually lives — and which kind of it is worth paying to amplify.

Issued September 2026 · Rev. 2

Part of the series: No. 01 The Venus Flytrap · No. 02 The Post Bank

The hard part

Nobody wants to follow an electrician

Start here, because everything else depends on accepting it: homeowners do not care about electrical work. They think about it roughly twice a decade, both times because something went wrong.

They don't follow trades on Facebook. They don't want to learn about your craft. They will never be impressed by a perfectly dressed panel, because they can't read it — to them it's a grey box with wires in it.

This is the single biggest mistake electricians make on social media. You post the work you're proud of, and you're proud of it for reasons a homeowner has no way of seeing.

Every post you make is competing with their kid's soccer photos. It has to earn the spot.

So stop posting about electrical. Start posting about their house.

The model

Fear, envy, suspicion

A homeowner stops scrolling on your post for one of three reasons. Every good post you will ever make runs on at least one of them. If a post doesn't hit one, don't post it.

Driver 01 — Fear
"Is that in my house?"

Something is hiding behind their walls and they have no way to know. This is the strongest driver and the easiest one to abuse.

Driver 02 — Envy
"I want my house to look like that."

Pure aspiration. Lighting, fixtures, outdoor. The only driver that makes people save your post instead of just reading it.

Driver 03 — Suspicion
"Am I getting ripped off?"

Every homeowner is quietly afraid of being taken by a trade. Answer that honestly and you win before you quote.

There's a fourth thing, but it's not a driver — it's a trigger. Timing. Storm season, a heat wave, the December breaker trip, the day they get the keys. The same post lands ten times harder the week it's relevant.

The big one

Where electrician virality actually lives

This matters more than any post idea in this guide, because it decides what's worth putting money behind. You never boost cold. You boost what already worked. So the question isn't "what should I post" — it's "what kind of working am I looking for."

Electrician content goes wide through five distinct doors. They are not equally valuable to you, and the ranking is going to be uncomfortable.

The door it goes throughReachBooks workWho it reaches
IdentityMemes that let an electrician say "this is me" by sharing it Electricians, everywhere
Shock"Look what I found behind this wall" Trade + rubberneckers + some homeowners
CraftSatisfying work — symmetry, repetition, precision Trade + the "oddly satisfying" audience
TransformationBefore and after, in a room a homeowner recognises Homeowners, tagging each other
LocalNamed streets, local housing stock, your utility People who can actually hire you

For a service electrician, reach and revenue pull in opposite directions. Everything that makes a post travel further makes it travel away from the people who can hire you.

That's not a clever line, it's the mechanism. A post spreads by being relatable to everyone. Your customers are defined by being within 30 minutes of your van. Those two forces fight, and the further a post travels the smaller the share of its audience that could ever book you.

The electricians you've seen go properly viral are in a different business

Look at who actually has the big followings in this trade. Ryan Davis (@rdaviselectrical) built past 300,000 followers. Aaron The Sparks, Electrician Barbie, and the rest of that tier all did the same thing. And the write-ups of them are unanimous about who's watching: prospective electricians, qualified electricians, and curious bystanders.

Almost none of them are homeowners, and virtually none live in the creator's service area. That audience buys tools, watches product reviews, and argues in the comments. It never books a service call.

Which is completely fine — they're running media businesses, monetised through sponsorship, product and brand deals. It's a real business and a good one. It just isn't the one your partners are running, and copying its content strategy into a service business is how a shop ends up with 40,000 followers and a quiet phone.

We do this on purpose — and it's the other business

Our own Million Dollar Electrician page is exactly that model. Our best post ever did 1,294,503 impressions and 3,635 shares off a trade meme. It worked because electricians are who we sell to. Run the identical play at a residential service shop and you get the numbers without the jobs.

So what do you actually chase?

Not reach. Local share rate. A post that reaches 600 households in your town and gets shared 20 times inside that town is worth more than one that reaches 600,000 strangers. It will look like a failure in your insights. It isn't.

Transformation content is the sweet spot and it's why your instinct about under-cabinet lighting was right. It travels through the one network that matters — homeowner to homeowner, usually as a tag, usually with "we should do this" attached. That tag is a referral with a photo attached.

The operating tool

Boost triage: reading a post before you spend

This is the bridge between this guide and No. 01. Everything you post organically is a free test. The job is reading the result correctly, because the headline numbers will lie to you.

The four signals, in order of usefulness

Share rate
The best single signal there is. Shares ÷ reach. A share means someone wanted a specific person to see it. Likes are politeness; shares are belief. Judge every post on this first.
Saves
Intent to act later. The strongest predictor of an eventual job, and the most under-read number on Facebook. High saves means "I'm going to want this."
Who commented
Open the commenters. Local names and questions about their own house? Boost it. Trade banter and tool arguments? It's a trade post, however good the numbers look.
Non-follower reach
Is it escaping your existing audience at all? If everything you post only reaches people who already follow you, boosting won't fix the content.

Then decide who to put money behind it with

High shares
local commenters
Boost cold, to your service radius. This is your Stage 1 magnet. It's already proven it can travel to strangers.
High saves
low shares
Boost warm, to followers only. It's a consideration asset, not a reach asset. Cheap, and it moves people toward calling.
Big reach
trade commenters
Don't boost it. You'd be paying to entertain electricians in other states. Enjoy the numbers, put it on your personal page, move on.
Flat everything
Don't boost it. Money doesn't fix content. A boost multiplies whatever the post already does — and multiplying zero gets you zero, slower.
The rule that saves the most money

Never boost something that hasn't already worked organically. Boosting is an amplifier, not an engine. Every dollar you put behind an unproven post is a dollar spent finding out what one free post would have told you.

Post four things. Let them sit a week. Boost the one that earned it. That's the whole discipline.

Supporting evidence

What your competitors are paying for right now

Organic tells you what people share. Ads tell you what people are willing to bet on. Worth a look as a cross-check — we pulled the live Facebook ad library for US electrical contractors running homeowner campaigns this month. Fifty-seven active ads, clustering into five angles.

Panel age
"Is Your Electrical Panel Outdated?" — self-diagnosis. Makes them go look at their own panel. — Panel Swap Electric
Fire
"Thousands of House Fires Start Like This" — pure fear hook. Widely used, easy to overcook. — Classic Electric Co
Free / rebate
"See If You Qualify for a Free Upgrade" · "Free Electrical Panel Inspection" — the most-copied angle in the whole set.
Instant price
"Instant EV Charger Install Estimate" — kills the "how much will this cost" anxiety up front. — Kurkov Electric
Anti-sell
"Before you spend thousands on a panel upgrade, make sure you actually need one." — the smartest one in the set. — Arc Angel Services

That last one is worth stealing outright. It leads with suspicion — it takes the homeowner's side against the industry, including against itself. You can't buy trust faster than by telling someone they might not need to buy anything.

One tactic in there you should not copy

Several of those ads run from pages called things like "Colorado Homeowner News" and "Utah Homeowner News" — lead-gen operations dressed up as local news outlets, funnelling to "Free Electrical Panel Inspection."

It works, and it is exactly the thing that makes homeowners distrust every electrician who comes after. You're building a local business with your actual name on it. Don't borrow credibility you didn't earn — you'll spend the next five years paying it back.

What this evidence is and isn't

The Ad Library shows what advertisers are running, not what's winning — there's no performance data attached. Repetition across many advertisers is a real signal that something works, but it's also partly everyone copying everyone. Treat it as a strong hint, not proof.

The fix nobody tells you

You're photographing for the wrong eyes

You take photos like an electrician. Your customer isn't one. Here's the translation.

What you shotWhat they seeShoot this instead
A beautifully dressed panel A grey box The same panel next to the one you pulled out. The contrast is the story, not the craft.
Close-up of a scorched breaker An unidentifiable burnt thing Pull back until the panel and the wall are in frame, so they can locate it in a real house.
A clean termination Nothing at all Skip it. This one is for your peers, not your market.
New fixture, straight on A light The whole room, lights off then on, camera locked in one position.
Conduit run, perfectly level Pipes Your hand or a tool in frame for scale, and the finished space behind it.

One rule covers most of it: get the room in the shot. A homeowner needs to recognise the space as a house like theirs before anything in it means something.

The bank · Driver 01

Fear — "is that in my house?"

Highest reach, highest share rate, easiest to get wrong. Every one of these must end by telling them something they can go and check themselves.

01"Go read the name on your panel door"

Zinsco, Federal Pacific, Challenger. Show what the label looks like, say what it means, tell them to go look. Specific beats scary.

Why it travels: it gives them a 30-second task. People comment with what they found, and every comment is a warm lead.
02The previous-owner special

Extension cord buried in a wall. Lamp cord feeding a garage. A splice with no box. Homeowners are all quietly worried about what the last guy did.

Tone: "this passed inspection in 1974" beats "this could kill your family."
03The five warning signs post

Flickering when the AC kicks on. Warm switch plates. Buzzing. Burning smell. Breakers that trip and trip. Plain list, no images needed.

Format: this is the single most saveable post in the whole guide. Make it a graphic they can screenshot.
04The space heater / power strip season post

Timed to first cold snap. What people daisy-chain every winter and what happens when they do.

05"This is what we found behind a nursery wall"

Kids' rooms raise the stakes honestly, without you having to say anything dramatic. Use sparingly and never as a gimmick.

06The smoke alarm age check

Almost nobody knows alarms expire. Show the date stamp on the back and tell them to flip theirs over.

Bonus: costs them nothing, helps them immediately, and half of them will find an expired one.
The line

Education and fearmongering look identical from ten feet. The difference is whether you tell them what to do. Ends with "check the label on your panel door" — education. Ends with "your family is at risk, call now" — fear, and they can smell it.

Get the facts straight. Electrical fires are a top-three cause of house fires, not number one. One commenter with Google will dismantle you in public.

The bank · Driver 02

Envy — "I want my house to look like that"

The only driver that gets you saved and sent to a spouse. Lower reach than fear, much higher intent. This is where under-cabinet lighting lives.

07Under-cabinet lighting, off → on

Locked-off camera, one frame dark, one lit. No talking, no explanation. The reveal is the entire post.

Shot: set the phone on the counter so both frames line up exactly. Alignment is what makes it satisfying.
08Builder-grade fixture → modern fixture

The boob light to a flush LED. Cheap job, huge visual delta, and the most relatable upgrade there is — almost every house has one.

09Exterior lighting at dusk

Soffit lighting, path lights, uplit trees. Shoot both frames at the same hour. This is the best-looking thing you will ever post.

10The dark room problem, solved

The gloomy hallway, the basement nobody uses, the kitchen with one ceiling light. Show the before honestly — ugly befores make better afters.

11The garage glow-up

Two bare bulbs to proper LED bays, plus outlets where they're actually needed. Enormously popular and usually a same-day job.

12Bathroom / vanity lighting

Bad lighting is the thing people hate most about their bathroom and can never name. Show them why.

13The "we hid it" post

TV mounted with no visible cord. EV charger run cleanly. Homeowners assume mess is unavoidable — proving it isn't sells the job.

14The outlet in the right place

Floor outlet under the couch. Outlet inside the vanity drawer. USB in the nightstand wall. Small, cheap, and people genuinely don't know they can ask for it.

The bank · Driver 03

Suspicion — "am I getting ripped off?"

Lowest reach of the three. Highest conversion by a distance, because it disarms the exact objection that stops someone calling.

15"Here's what this actually cost"

A real job, a real number, and the breakdown. Nobody does this. The first electrician in your market who does owns the conversation.

Nerve: yes, competitors will see it. They already know your prices. Homeowners don't, and that's the gap costing you calls.
16"You probably don't need a panel upgrade"

When a panel is fine, say so publicly. Explain when it genuinely isn't. Takes their side against your own industry and buys enormous trust.

17"Three questions to ask any electrician before you hire them"

License, insurance, who's actually doing the work. You'll pass all three. Most of the cheap guy won't.

18Why the cheap quote was cheap

What gets left out — permits, materials, making good, warranty. Explain the mechanism, never name a competitor.

19"What a permit actually does for you"

Most homeowners think a permit is a tax. Explain that it's an independent inspection they get for free, and cheap unpermitted work stops looking cheap.

20The honest DIY post

Something they genuinely can do themselves — swapping a fixture, resetting a GFCI — and the clear line where it stops. Giving away the easy one proves you're not grabbing.

The bank · Trigger

Timing — the same post, ten times harder

21The storm-week post

Written and ready before the forecast. Generator interlocks, what not to backfeed, what to do when it comes back on. Post it the day it's news.

22First heat wave / AC load

Why the lights dim when the AC starts, and when that's normal versus when it isn't.

23Holiday lighting, early

Posted in October, not December. Outdoor outlets, timers, why the breaker trips every year.

24"Just bought a house?"

The five electrical things to check in the first month. New owners are the highest-intent audience in your entire market and they're actively looking.

25The rebate deadline

A real program with a real expiry. Urgency you didn't have to manufacture.

The multiplier

Local beats clever, every time

You are not trying to go viral. You are trying to be known by a few thousand people who live within 30 minutes of your van. A post that reaches 400 of the right households beats one that reaches 400,000 of the wrong ones.

26Name the neighbourhood

"We were in [subdivision] this week." People who live there will find it, and it quietly tells everyone else you work in good areas.

27The housing-stock post

"If your house was built between 1965 and 1975 around here, this is what's in your walls." Hyper-local, instantly relevant, impossible for a national page to copy.

28The local utility explainer

Your actual power company, their actual programs, their actual process for a service upgrade. Nobody else is writing this.

29Sponsorships and community

The team, the fundraiser, the food drive. Local credibility compounds in a way advertising can't buy.

30Answer the neighbourhood group question

When someone asks for an electrician recommendation in a local group, answer helpfully without pitching. Then post the same answer on your page.

Note: your page content and your presence in local groups feed each other. The group builds the reputation; the page holds it.

How to write it

Caption rules for people who aren't electricians

No jargon
Not "double-tapped breaker." Say "two wires crammed under one screw that's only built for one."
First line
Has to work alone — it's all most people see before "see more."
Their house
Second person, always. "Your panel," not "this panel." "If your house was built before…"
One takeaway
Something they can check, do, or look at today. Every post earns its spot by being useful once.
No links
Outbound links get buried. If you need one, put it in the first comment.
Close
A question, or "send this to someone who just bought a house." People share to look helpful.

Failure modes

Five ways homeowner posts die

  1. Posting for other electricians. If the only people who'd appreciate it own a set of Kleins, it's the wrong post for this page. Put it on your personal profile instead.
  2. Hazard photos with no takeaway. Without "here's what to check," it reads as fear. With it, it's the most useful thing in their feed that day.
  3. Jargon. The moment they have to look something up, they're gone.
  4. Selling in every post. Stage 1 and 2 don't ask for anything. If every post has a phone number, nobody follows the page.
  5. Only posting finished work. Nice photos of completed jobs are the lowest-performing content in this entire guide. The before is what makes the after mean anything.

Honest limits

Prove this on your own page in 30 days

Where this comes from

Measured: our own page numbers — 1,294,503 impressions and 3,635 shares on one trade post, and what that reach did and didn't convert into. That's real, and it's what the reach-versus-revenue argument rests on.

Documented: the follower counts and audience make-up of the big electrician creators, and the five live ad angles from Facebook's public ad library. The ad library shows where money is going, not which ads are winning.

Reasoned, not measured: the 30 posts and the boost triage thresholds. We don't run a residential service page, so nobody should hand you this as proven. It's a strong starting list and a sound way to read your own numbers — not a scoreboard.

Which is fine, because you can settle it yourself faster than anyone could settle it for you:

  • Pick one post from each driver — one fear, one envy, one suspicion. Post one a week, unboosted.
  • Judge on shares and saves, not likes. Likes are politeness; shares are belief.
  • Whichever driver wins on your page, that's your market's temperament. Make three more of those.
  • Then put the $10/day behind the single best one and run the Venus Flytrap.

Inside a month you'll know more about what your neighbourhood shares than any guide could tell you — and this list becomes a backup you've outgrown.
