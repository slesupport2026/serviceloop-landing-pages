What do I say when a customer wants the quote or options emailed over instead of deciding in person?
Asked 89×Objection HandlingpartnerJoseph2023-09-13 – 2026-10-15
▶
Don't fight it and don't refuse it. Move the conversation to their timeline, then ask which option they'd like emailed — that turns a stall into a decision.

The mistake: arguing about email. You can't win that, and you look like you're hiding something.

What to do instead — two moves.

1. Redirect to their date, not yours.

"Just so I know how to serve you best — when were you hoping to have all this taken care of?"

Ask when they want it done, never when they're finishing their proposals. Their timeline is a real conversation. Your competitors' timeline is not your business and asking about it benefits you, not them.

2. Ask which one.

If you've presented six options, "email it over" has an easy answer:

"Absolutely — which option would you like me to email you?"

That single question forces clarity. They either name one, which means you're closing, or they admit they haven't decided, which puts you back in a conversation.

Why in person matters, and how to say it. The presentation is part of what they're buying:

"We are providing a service. The before is the design of the options and the presentation. The during is the work. The after is the follow-up and the warranty. It's a whole experience — if you take away part of the experience, you're not getting everything you paid for."

You can say that to them, out loud, exactly that way.

If the job genuinely isn't scoped yet, don't guess on paper. Book the evaluation:

"Let's schedule a time where I'm at your home for a few hours. I'll do a thorough evaluation and have your options designed and ready to present. This way we're not guessing — we're building you something custom."

The root cause. Most "email it over" requests happen because one price was presented. One choice is no choice — it's an ultimatum, and email is how a homeowner escapes an ultimatum politely. Six options removes the reason to ask.

What do I say when a customer tells me my price is too high?
Asked 61×Objection HandlingpartnerJoseph2023-11-02 – 2026-10-01
▶
Acknowledge it without apologising, reframe onto what they're actually buying, then ask again. Never discount before they ask, and never discount first.

Price objections are usually surprise, not inability. Most homeowners have no idea what it costs to run a licensed electrical business.

The framework: Acknowledge → Reframe → Ask Again.

Acknowledge. Don't flinch and don't defend:

"I understand this may be more than you were expecting."

Reframe onto value, not parts:

"What you're paying for is reliability, safety, and the peace of mind that the job is done right the first time."

Expand into the before, during and after — the evaluation, the design, the presentation, the workmanship, the warranty, the follow-up. That's the product. The wire is not the product.

Ask again. Go back to the options. You have six for exactly this reason:

"Which of these feels like the right fit for your home?"

Two things to never do.

  1. Never discount before they ask. #STYO — Stick To Your Offer. An unconfident price sounds like an apology, and an apology sounds like incompetence.
  2. Never devalue the service to justify the number. If you talk your own work down, they'll believe you.

The internal version of this objection is the real one. If you don't believe your price, the homeowner hears it in your tone before they hear the number. Two questions to settle it:

"If you weren't an electrician, would you hire this company at full price to help with a safety concern?"
"So why do you think other people hire us then?"

And remember the pattern. Once you've charged your true rate a few times and people keep saying yes, the shock fades. The same customer who thought $30/hr was too high will hire you at $300/hr once they trust the experience.

How do I generate more leads when my lead flow is low or inconsistent?
Asked 58×Marketing & Lead GenpartnerForrest2023-06-16 – 2025-11-14
▶
Run three lead engines at once — paid, organic and referral — and fix speed-to-lead first. The first company to call back wins 78% of the time.

Inconsistent lead flow is almost always a one-engine problem. Most electricians either spend everything on ads and pray, or live on word-of-mouth and hope. Neither is a business model.

Run three engines at the same time. When one slows, the others carry.

Engine 1 — Paid (fast, controllable, costs money).

  • Google Local Service Ads — top of "electrician near me." Google rewards fast response.
  • Meta ads — post organic video first, find what performs, then put spend behind the proven posts.
  • Lead aggregators (Angi, Thumbtack) — good training ground for speed-to-lead. Dorian Hayes turned a $24 Angi lead into $66,700 because his system was good, not because the lead was.

Engine 2 — Organic (slow start, compounds forever).

  • Facebook personal profile, 4–5 posts a week. Most work and most hires come from second- and third-degree connections.
  • Google Business Profile, complete, with photos. Target 100 reviews to start, then 1,000.
  • SEO — a marathon. Start now, stay consistent.

Engine 3 — Referral (highest quality, most ignored).

  • Van wraps, 5 door hangers at every job, a yard sign at every completed job, photos and a review ask every time.
  • Database reactivation — your best lead source is already in your phone. Work unsold leads up to 10 times before you close the file. They're waiting for help, not lost.
  • Happy calls after every job, made by your CSR rather than the tech.

Fix this before you spend a dollar: speed-to-lead. The first company to call back wins 78% of the time — not the cheapest, not the most experienced, the fastest. Close rate halves by the 30-minute mark. If you're on a job when the phone rings, that's a CSR problem, not a marketing problem.

Then score your leads so the best tech runs the best calls — 5-star (in area, responsive, ready, multiple needs) down to 1-star (time wasters, no decision authority). Don't run 1-star calls at all.

Start where the cost is zero. Nathan Miles did $75,005 in his first full month with zero ad spend — BNI, referrals and conversations. The system came before the leads. That's the order that matters.

How do I justify my price when a customer compares me to a cheaper competitor?
Asked 36×Objection HandlingpartnerJoseph2023-09-13 – 2026-10-06
▶
Never ask them to pay more for less. Name everything included in your price and invite them to ask the cheaper quote what it leaves out.

The line that does the work:

"I would never ask you to pay more for less. What I can tell you is that when you work with us, you're getting the evaluation, the design, the presentation, the warranty and the follow-up. If someone's offering you less, it's worth asking what part of that experience they're leaving out."

That reframes the comparison without ever criticising the other electrician. The moment you run a competitor down, you look threatened.

Why this works. The homeowner is comparing two numbers because nobody has given them anything else to compare. Your job is to put more on the table than price:

  • A lifetime craftsmanship warranty in writing
  • Six options so they control the budget, not you
  • A full inspection and a designed solution, not a guessed number
  • One price with nothing added at checkout — no "taxes and fees not included," no card surcharge

Build the offer so the comparison stops happening. The six pillars of an irresistible offer are sustainable pricing, trip charges, club memberships, value drivers, six options, and lifetime warranty plus financing. When those are in place, homeowners stop comparing you to the cheap quote — there's nothing to compare it to.

Use the warranty tier break. Everything from Economy up carries the lifetime guarantee. Below that is explicitly a temporary fix:

"I can't guarantee a temporary solution for life."

That gives the cheaper quote a category, and it isn't the category most people want for their home.

Last thing: don't prejudge. Appearance, neighbourhood and accent do not predict buying behaviour. Plenty of homes that "looked like" a cheap-quote house have taken the top option. Give everyone the dignity of the full choice.

How do I decide on joining SLE with my spouse or business partner?

Bring them into the conversation rather than relaying it. Decide together what the next 12 months need to look like, and use the Deal Formula — raise trust and desire, lower risk and cost.

This is the most-asked question on the prospect side, and the honest answer is: don't go away and sell it for us.

Get them on the call. A decision made in the kitchen afterwards is a decision made with half the information and none of the energy. If your spouse or business partner shares the risk, they should hear it first-hand and get to ask their own questions. We would rather run the conversation twice than have you relay it once.

What they're really asking. Their questions are almost never about the curriculum. They are:

  • Can we afford this right now, honestly?
  • What happens to our family or our cash if it doesn't work?
  • Is this the thing that finally changes something, or is it another thing we buy and don't use?

Those are fair questions. Answer them out loud together.

The Deal Formula:

(Trust × Desire) ÷ (Risk × Cost) = Partnership

If the decision is stuck, one of those four is off. Usually it's perceived risk. That's what the guarantees exist for — the Kickspark $6K rolls forward into the next tier, and Guaranteed Results is money-back if you don't get the $30K+ sales improvement.

Three questions to decide on together:

  1. What does the next 12 months look like if nothing changes?
  2. What would have to be true for this to be the best money we spend this year?
  3. What's the real number we can commit without putting the house under pressure?

What we won't do. We won't pressure a partner into a decision their spouse hasn't agreed to. Partnerships that start that way don't last, and we're building a ten-year relationship, not a transaction.

How do I find time to run and grow my business when I'm still doing the selling, installing, and admin work myself?
Asked 25×Operations & AdminpartnerClay2023-07-12 – 2026-10-13
▶
You're at 50–60% of your growth capacity while overwhelmed. Sort every open loop into do / delegate / drop, eat the frog first, and delegate one thing a week — starting with the phone.

The cost of staying here. Overwhelmed, you run at 50–60% of your growth capacity. Organised, you run at 90–100%. Across one year that gap is more than 100 days of lost productivity. You're not short of hours. You're short of offense.

Step 1 — Empty your head onto paper. Write three lists: Health open cycles, Relationship open cycles, Business open cycles. Every unfinished thing in your head is an open cycle and every open cycle drains energy whether you're working on it or not.

Step 2 — Label every item 1, 2 or 3.

  1. Must do myself — genuinely only you, right now
  2. Must delegate — outside your specialty or better done by someone else
  3. Must eat crow — commitments, habits or relationships you need to cut loose

Step 3 — Work the list in this order.

  • Eat the frog. Hardest and shortest item first, every morning, before anything else.
  • Delegate one item per week, starting with the one with the highest revenue impact.
  • Eat crow on one item every day until the list is empty.

Step 4 — Hire the phone, not the tools. Your first hire is a CSR, not a tech. If a CSR prevents 5 missed calls a week and each is worth $1,500 in pipeline, that's $7,500/week you're currently losing. A CSR costs $3,000–$4,000/month. Nathan Miles's revenue accelerated the month he hired his CSR — not because he got more leads, but because he stopped losing the ones he had.

"While I'm doing installs, no growth is happening to my company." — Nathan Miles

Step 5 — The 72-hour rule. Any decision that isn't made within 72 hours costs you momentum. Make it, or take it off the list.

The honest diagnosis. Two things keep electricians at "self-employed" instead of "entrepreneur": discipline (the right things, for the right people, at the right times) and trust (the willingness to let someone else own something). The time problem is almost always the trust problem wearing a disguise.

How do I get my team (or myself) to consistently present the full set of options instead of settling for one price, and raise average ticket by doing it?
Asked 25×Sales & ClosingpartnerJoseph2023-07-31 – 2026-05-21
▶
Make six options the standard, not the stretch goal — build bottom-up, price top-down, present top-down, and inspect it weekly. Most 'we do options' shops are actually doing two.

Why it has to be six. One option is an ultimatum. Three options ("good, better, best") still gives each buyer type exactly one real choice — still an ultimatum, just dressed up. Six covers every buyer:

  • 2 premium — Platinum, Gold
  • 2 mid-range — Silver, Bronze
  • 2 economy — Economy, Basic

Platinum isn't meant to be sold; it sets the ceiling. Basic isn't meant to be sold either; it proves you're informing, not pushing.

The mechanics that make it repeatable.

Build from the bottom up. Price from the top down. Start the build at the immediate need and layer upward. Start the presentation at Platinum and come down. Each step down should drop 15–30% — consistent gaps. A jump from $4,200 to $1,500 creates a mental hole and the customer starts wondering what they're missing.

Use "same as above." Full description on Platinum, then "same as above, without X" for Gold. Full description on Silver, "same as above, without Y" for Bronze. Each pair becomes easy to compare and you stop rewriting the same lines.

Hide the price until value is built. Present all six without numbers first. Let them react to the solutions. Then reveal pricing. At that point price is the last objection, because every other one is already resolved.

Getting the team to actually do it. This is where most shops fail. Our Offers scoring rule exists because of it:

Options Score = MIN(options presented, AVERAGE(tiers clear + tech can present without owner))

That's deliberately hard to game. "We do six options" means nothing if it only happens when you're in the van.

What actually moves it:

  1. Weekly option-building sessions. Treat designing options as technical training, not sales training — it genuinely improves install knowledge and confidence.
  2. A blank tablet template, not your CRM. Faster, more flexible, keeps you present with the customer.
  3. Inspect it. Review real option sheets in a huddle. Coach the gap. An expectation you don't inspect is a suggestion.
  4. Pay for it. Tie performance pay to average ticket and close rate so the behaviour has a reason to stick.

What it's worth. One partner's first three presentations with a service rate instead of a price book: a Basic at $694 that would previously have been $225, plus Gold options at $2,794 and $2,523. Three calls, $6,011 against roughly $675 under the old approach.

How do I handle it when the decision-maker isn't present for the appointment?
Asked 23×Sales & ClosingpartnerJoseph2024-04-16 – 2026-09-01
▶
Prevent it at the booking, not at the door. If you're already there, run the full evaluation, build the options, and schedule the presentation when both people can be present.

Fix this at the front door — the phone call. Your CSR should confirm at booking that everyone involved in the decision will be there. It's a service framing, not a screening one:

"So that we can go over everything properly and nobody has to hear it second-hand — will both of you be home at that time?"

If you arrive and they're not there, don't present. A presentation to half a household gets relayed badly and dies. Instead:

  1. Do the full evaluation anyway. The inspection is real work and it's what you came for.
  2. Gather the absent person's perspective through the one who's there — what matters to them, what they've noticed, what they've been worried about. You'll need their words in the observations.
  3. Design the options.
  4. Book the presentation when both can be there:
"I've got everything I need and I'm going to have your options designed and ready. The last thing I want is for you to have to explain my recommendations for me — when's a time this week you're both around for twenty minutes?"

Write both people into the observations. When you do present, name them:

"Doug and Emma expressed multiple priorities, including safety upgrades, lighting improvements, and future power needs for the shed."

That one sentence tells both of them they were heard.

The realtor version. If a realtor is arranging work for a buyer, don't email the realtor a quote. The realtor isn't the decision-maker. Set the expectation that you'll meet the actual buyer to go over scope and pricing, and present to them directly.

And if they insist you present anyway? Present, then close the loop yourself rather than leaving it to be relayed — offer to call the absent partner right there, or book the follow-up before you leave. Don't walk out with the decision in someone else's hands and no appointment.

How do I manage my time and delegate better so I'm not drowning in work as the business grows?
Asked 23×Operations & AdminpartnerClay2023-11-17 – 2026-07-30
▶
Delegation fails on trust, not on time. Pick one item a week, hand over the whole outcome rather than the task, and write the SOP as you go.

Start with the diagnosis. Two things keep electricians stuck at self-employed: discipline and trust. If you've read the time-management advice and you're still drowning, it's almost never that you don't know what to delegate. It's that you don't believe anyone will do it properly.

The weekly rhythm.

1. Sort every open cycle into: must do myself / must delegate / must eliminate. Be ruthless about the third list — it's usually the longest and it's free to clear.

2. Delegate one item per week. One. Start with whatever has the highest revenue impact that you're doing unnecessarily. In a year that's 52 things off your plate, which is a different business.

3. Delegate the outcome, not the task. "Book the Tuesday calls" is a task and you'll still own it. "You own the schedule — here's what a good week looks like" is an outcome, and it transfers.

4. Write the SOP while they learn it. Have the person doing the work write the steps down as you train them. You get documentation for free, they get ownership, and the next hire costs a fraction to onboard.

5. Inspect, don't hover. ITRS — Inspect, Train, Recognise, Support. Set the KPI, look at it weekly, coach the gap. Checking the number is not the same as doing the job for them.

Order of operations if you're solo. CSR first, always. They take the phone, the scheduling, the follow-up and the happy calls — which is most of what's drowning you — and they pay for themselves on missed calls alone.

The test you're aiming for. Can the business run for two weeks without you? Can your techs sell without you coaching every call? If not, you don't have a business yet, you have a job with employees. That's not an insult — it's the specific thing the next 90 days need to fix.

What do I say when a customer wants to think it over before deciding?
Asked 23×Objection HandlingpartnerJoseph2023-09-25 – 2025-09-03
▶
'Think it over' is a missing piece, not a no. Ask what they want to think about, answer that, and anchor on when they want the work done — then book the follow-up before you leave.

What it actually means. Nobody needs to think over a decision they fully understand and can afford. "Let me think about it" means one of three things: I don't understand something, I can't afford this one, or I'm not the only decider. Your job is to find out which.

The question:

"Absolutely — what part of it do you want to think about most?"

Said warmly, this almost always gets a real answer. Then handle that objection, which is the one you were actually facing.

The timeline redirect. Don't ask when they'll decide. Ask when they want it done:

"When were you hoping to bring this to a conclusion?"
"When did you want to have all this taken care of?"

Their project date is a real constraint you can work with. Their decision date is a date they invented to end the conversation.

Then make the next step concrete. Never leave with "give me a call." Leave with a booked time:

"How about I check in Thursday at 10? If it's a no by then, just say so and I'll close the file — you won't hear from me again."

Giving them permission to say no is what makes them answer the phone.

The two-call close is legitimate. For big or unclear jobs, a deliberate second visit beats a rushed decision — evaluate now, present properly next week. That's not losing the sale, it's structuring it.

And if it still goes unsold: it goes into the unsold follow-up process, framed as quality control rather than a second pitch. Many "not today" customers simply needed more time, a conversation with a spouse, or a pay cheque. That revenue is deferred, not gone — and it belongs to whoever follows up first.

How do I build a full set of pricing options on a small, simple job?
Asked 22×Pricing & OptionspartnerJoseph2024-05-30 – 2026-04-24
▶
Small jobs are where the full option set matters most. Layer by safety priority, scope, materials and future-proofing — and if you can only honestly build four, build four and label them correctly.

The trap. "It's just a GFI" is how a $300 call stays a $300 call. The home has more than one electrical need — it always does — and the only question is whether you looked.

Four axes to build on when the job itself is small:

  1. Fix the fault — exactly what they called you for, nothing else. That's Basic.
  2. Method and materials — same fix, done better. Surface mount vs recessed in the wall. Residential grade vs commercial grade. Patched vs painted and finished.
  3. Same problem, whole house — one GFI becomes every non-GFI location that should have one: bathrooms, exterior, laundry, kitchen. Outlets take priority over switches, and anything near water takes priority over everything.
  4. The system behind it — the panel, the circuit, the protection. If the panel feeding it is a Federal Pacific, that's an option, not a footnote.

Joseph's junction-box example, start to finish: Basic is running the power surface mount, 20 amp into the junction. Economy is the same thing recessed within the wall. Above that, you're not changing the task, you're changing the capacity — running a 240V 30-amp line instead. They may never need 60 amps total, but if it's ever going to happen, now is when the wall is open.

Then add what's genuinely available: whole-home electronics protection, circuit identification and labelling, a safety inspection, the First Class membership. These are real services, not padding.

If you honestly can't build six, don't fake it. Build four and label them for what they are:

  • Building bottom-up from a small fault → Gold, Silver, Bronze, Economy
  • Building top-heavy → start at Gold and come down

The labels must match what you're actually delivering. A "Platinum" that's a GFI and a handshake destroys the ladder for every future job.

Language matters more on small jobs than big ones. "Install a duplex outlet" invites a hardware-store price comparison. "Upgrading your home's electrical points for safer power distribution" doesn't. Describe what the part does, never what it is.

How do I fix an unstable or tight cash flow — debt, feast-or-famine deposits, no reserve, or seasonal slow periods?
Asked 18×Money & FinancialspartnerClay2023-06-21 – 2026-02-24
▶
Run four buckets — operating, tax, profit, growth — and a Monday cash rhythm. Then fix the two causes: no deposits, and no recurring revenue to carry the slow months.

Cash is oxygen. Revenue on paper means nothing if you can't make payroll, and most electricians judge their position by looking at one bank balance and feeling either rich or panicked.

The Cash Bucket System. Split collected revenue four ways:

  • Operating — payroll, materials, fuel, the week's expenses
  • Tax — 25–30% of profit, set aside before you touch anything
  • Profit — yours. Take it monthly. Don't reinvest all of it
  • Growth — funded by the Growth Rate already in your SSR, spent only on planned expansion

The Monday rhythm. Every week, same order:

  1. Review last week's collected revenue
  2. Allocate to the four buckets
  3. Review outstanding invoices and chase them
  4. Ask: can I make payroll? Can I cover this week's materials?
  5. If a bucket is low, that's a signal — adjust now, before it's a crisis

The three numbers to watch: Days Sales Outstanding (how fast you collect), cash conversion cycle (how fast revenue becomes usable cash), and weekly collection rate — target 90%+ of invoiced work collected within 7 days.

Now the two root causes.

1. You're floating the job. If you're buying materials and paying labour before you see money, you've lent the customer your working capital. Standard is a 50% deposit on projects — it covers materials, part of labour and part of profit. The Casino Principle: structure it so that if the client walks, your costs are still covered. Where deposits are legally limited, phase the job with payment milestones at design, permitting, material ordering and installation.

2. You have no recurring revenue. Feast-or-famine is a membership problem. The First Class Service Club — front of the line, after-hours access, waived diagnostics, annual safety inspection — at $250–$450 a year with ~30% adoption gives you a base that doesn't care what the season is. Pair it with warranty follow-ups scheduled deliberately into November–February and the slow season becomes the maintenance season.

On debt. Debt is a symptom here, not the disease. The disease is almost always underpricing plus floating jobs. Fix the rate and the deposits and the hole stops getting deeper — then you can plan your way out of the rest with a reverse P&L.

How do I calculate my SSR so it truly covers all my costs (labor, overhead, CSR/future hires, profit)?
Asked 17×Pricing & OptionspartnerClay2023-09-20 – 2026-10-20
▶
Burden + Growth + Profit + Material = your Simple Service Rate. Divide real annual costs by 1,000 billable hours per van, add growth and 30%, then add the material rate.

Four stages. Do them in order and don't skip one.

Stage 1 — Burden Rate. What does it cost to run the business for one billable hour?

  1. Pull total annual expenses from your accounting software or P&L.
  2. Include everything, especially the things owners leave out: your own salary, retirement/investment (10% is the recommendation), and a 90-day nest egg.
  3. Count billable units — each active van is one unit. Only count vans you have today.
  4. Apply the 50% rule. A full-time year is ~2,000 hours but only about half is billable after admin, travel, training and downtime. Use 1,000 billable hours per unit.
$236,000 ÷ 1,000 = $236/hr burden rate

The mistake that kills this: projecting future vans or future hires into the model. It drops your rate and destroys your margin before those people exist. Price the business you have.

Stage 2 — Growth Rate. What it costs to get where you're going: new vans, wraps and branding, marketing, training, raises, website and tech. Look at your 1–3 year goals, cost them, divide. Most businesses land at $50–$150 per billable hour.

"It is always better to be looking at the money than looking for it."

Stage 3 — Profit. Combine burden + growth, multiply by 0.30, add it back.

$236 + $90 = $326. +30% ($98) = $424/hr

Note: 30% markup is not a 30% margin. It realises roughly 23% net once everything is counted.

Stage 4 — Material Rate. Consumables that leave the van but never get billed — wire nuts, screws, boxes, connectors, plates, spackle. Across hundreds of service electricians this lands consistently at $50–$75/hr.

$424 + $50 = $474/hr Simple Service Rate

Sanity check. A correct rate almost always lands between $250 and $500 per billable hour. If you're under $250, something is missing — usually your own salary, the nest egg, or you've counted vans you don't own yet.

Then use it: SSR × hours + specialty materials + third-party (marked up 20–30%) + permits = price. A 10-hour panel replacement at $474 is $4,740 before arc-fault breakers, surge protection and permits, which go on top.

On the number feeling wrong. Trust your math more than your emotions. Price shock is the discomfort of a number being new to you — it isn't new to the market. Review the whole thing twice a year and after every annual budget.

(Trust × Desire) ÷ (Risk × Cost) = Partnership

The calculator is four inputs in sequence. Almost every wrong answer comes from the same handful of mistakes, so here's the setup with the traps marked.

$236,000 ÷ 1,000 = $236/hr

($236 + $90) × 1.30 = $424/hr
